Lesson 4 · Processing, labels & food choices

Understand how dairy is made safer and more stable—and how to read the claims on packages.

Big idea: Processing can reduce microbes, slow spoilage, change texture, or adjust composition. Each process has a particular purpose; “processed” does not mean unsafe, and a package claim only means what its definition says.

1. Why milk needs preservation

Milk is nutrient-rich and contains water, so microbes can grow in it if it is contaminated and kept warm. Preservation uses one or more hurdles: clean handling, heat treatment, cold storage, removing water, fermentation, packaging, or combinations of these. Refrigeration slows microbial growth; it does not kill every microbe. Drying removes water needed for growth. Acid from fermentation changes the environment. Pasteurization reduces harmful microbes and many spoilage organisms, but pasteurized milk still needs refrigeration unless it has received a shelf-stable treatment and been aseptically packaged.

2. Pasteurization and UHT

Pasteurization is a controlled time-and-temperature heat process chosen to destroy target disease-causing microbes and reduce spoilage organisms while retaining useful food quality. The exact required treatment depends on the product and governing standard. Common teaching examples for fluid milk include a batch treatment near 63°C (145°F) for 30 minutes, and high-temperature short-time (HTST) near 72°C (161°F) for 15 seconds. Do not treat any one pair as universal for every dairy product or country; use the event rules or current regulator for exact requirements.

Ultra-high-temperature (UHT) processing heats milk to a higher temperature for a very short time, then packages it under sterile conditions. The product can remain shelf-stable unopened. Once opened, it needs refrigeration. Pasteurized and UHT milk are not the same as completely sterile food in an ordinary package; safe packaging and storage matter.

4. Lactose-free dairy and plant-based drinks

Lactose-free cow's milk is usually made by adding the enzyme lactase, which splits lactose into glucose and galactose. Those simpler sugars taste sweeter, so lactose-free milk may taste sweeter even without extra table sugar. Its protein, milk allergy risk, and dairy identity remain. Some products reduce lactose by filtration or other processing.

Plant-based beverages can be made from soy, oats, almonds, rice, coconut, peas, or other ingredients. They differ widely in protein, energy, fat, carbohydrate, and added sugar. Some are fortified with calcium and vitamin D; others are not. Soy beverage often has more protein than many nut or grain drinks, but actual products vary. Compare the Nutrition Facts and ingredient list rather than assuming “milk” alternatives have cow's milk's composition. For a dairy allergy, check the exact product and cross-contact statement.

5. Reading dairy labels

  1. Serving size: the amounts and %DV refer to the listed serving. Compare equivalent serving sizes.
  2. Calories and nutrients: see energy, total/saturated fat, carbohydrate, total and added sugars, protein, sodium, and required vitamins/minerals.
  3. Percent Daily Value: FDA's general guide is 5% DV or less = low; 20% DV or more = high for that nutrient in one serving. This is a quick label guide, not an individualized nutrition prescription.
  4. Ingredients: ingredients are generally listed in descending order by weight. The allergen declaration identifies milk when present as a major allergen.
  5. Claims: “low fat,” “fat free,” “lactose free,” “organic,” and “non-dairy” describe different properties. Do not infer one claim from another.

RDA vs %DV: an RDA is an age- and life-stage-specific recommended intake used in nutrition science. FDA %DV is a standard label reference used to compare foods, and it may not equal a particular child's RDA. For example, calcium's label DV is 1,300 mg; the RDA is 1,300 mg for ages 9–18 but 1,000 mg for many adults.

Some nutrient claims have legal thresholds. For general U.S. packaged-food claims, “fat free” means less than 0.5 g fat per reference amount customarily consumed (RACC) and labeled serving; “low fat” is 3 g or less per RACC (with an additional condition for small-RACC foods); “reduced fat” means at least 25% less fat than an appropriate reference food. The comparison and serving basis matter. Dairy foods can also be subject to product-specific standards, so use current rules for a product’s regulated identity.

6. USDA organic claims

USDA organic is a regulated production and handling certification, not a synonym for “local,” “natural,” “pesticide-free,” or “more nutritious.” Multi-ingredient package categories depend on the share of certified organic ingredients (water and salt are excluded): “100% organic” means all ingredients qualify; “organic” requires at least 95%; “made with organic …” requires at least 70% and follows additional restrictions. The USDA seal may appear on eligible “100% organic” and “organic” products, but not products in the “made with” category.

For organic dairy, USDA standards require organic feed and forage, prohibit antibiotics and hormones in organic livestock management, and require pasture-based management for ruminants. They must graze during a season of at least 120 days and receive at least 30% of their dry-matter feed intake from pasture during that grazing season. If an animal needs a prohibited medical treatment, animal welfare comes first, but its product cannot continue to be sold as organic. Consult the current USDA standard for compliance details.

7. Milk as an allergen

U.S. rules identify milk as a major food allergen. The concern is protein derived from milk, so milk ingredients must be disclosed as required by law. A milk allergy is an immune reaction and can be severe. Lactose intolerance is a problem digesting the milk sugar; it is not an allergy. A “lactose-free” product can still trigger a milk allergy. In the U.S., some foods called “non-dairy” (especially creamers) may contain a milk-derived caseinate ingredient; milk must still be identified as an allergen when required. “Non-dairy” by itself is not a safety guarantee. Read the ingredient list and “Contains” statement; for an allergy, follow medical advice and the current FDA guidance.
