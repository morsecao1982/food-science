Lesson 1 · Milk is a molecular system

Learn what milk contains and why one liquid can behave like a solution, a colloid, and an emulsion at once.

Big idea: Milk is not a single chemical. It is mostly water carrying dissolved molecules and tiny suspended particles. Its structure explains cream rising, milk looking white, and the way dairy responds to mixing, acid, heat, and freezing.

1. What is in milk?

Cow's milk is roughly 87% water. Most of the rest is milkfat, lactose (milk sugar), proteins, and minerals. A useful approximate composition from dairy science is water 87.3%, fat 3.9%, protein 3.25%, lactose about 4.6%, and minerals about 0.7%; breed, feed, season, and processing change the exact values. For competition questions, learn the parts and what they do rather than memorizing one universal recipe.

2. Solution, colloid, emulsion: all three

A solution has substances dissolved as individual molecules or ions in another substance. Milk's lactose and many mineral ions are dissolved in its water. A colloid has particles small enough to remain dispersed for a long time without simply dissolving; casein micelles are colloidal protein particles. An emulsion is a mixture of droplets of one liquid dispersed in another liquid that normally does not mix with it. Milkfat droplets are dispersed through watery milk, so milk is an oil-in-water emulsion.

The fat-globule membrane contains proteins and phospholipids. It helps keep globules separate. A phospholipid has a water-attracting region and a water-avoiding region, letting it sit at the boundary between fat and water. This is why it can help stabilize an emulsion.

Picture it: Imagine a glass of milk as water containing invisible dissolved sugar, tiny casein “beads,” and fat droplets with protective coats. The milk appears uniform to your eyes, even though a microscope would reveal several different structures.

3. Dairying and food traditions

Humans began keeping milk animals thousands of years ago. Dairying gave people a way to turn grasses and other animal feed into a steady food supply without slaughtering the animal. Communities found ways to preserve milk or change its texture: separation produces cream; churning makes butter; acidification makes cultured milk and helps form curds; draining and aging produce many kinds of cheese. These are examples of people using physical processes and microbes to transform a perishable food.

Food history also explains why dairy differs around the world. People raised different animals—cattle, goats, sheep, buffalo, yak, and camels—and used local microbes, temperatures, and storage practices. So dairy science is both chemistry and biology applied to cultural food traditions.

4. Vocabulary to know

  • Emulsion: droplets of one liquid dispersed in another immiscible liquid.
  • Emulsifier: a molecule that helps the two phases stay dispersed.
  • Colloid: tiny particles dispersed through a medium; they do not settle quickly.
  • Continuous phase: the material surrounding the dispersed droplets or particles.
  • Homogenization: processing that breaks milkfat globules into smaller ones and helps keep them dispersed.
  • Fat-soluble vitamins: vitamins A, D, E, and K, which associate with fat more readily than water.
