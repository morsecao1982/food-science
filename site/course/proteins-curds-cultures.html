Lesson 3 · Proteins, curds & cultures

Follow milk proteins from tiny particles to curds, yogurt, cheese, and experimental measurements.

Big idea: Milk proteins behave differently. Acid can make casein proteins gather into curds, while many whey proteins stay in the liquid. Microbes can acidify milk and preserve it by changing its chemistry.

1. Protein structure in plain language

Proteins are long chains made from amino acids. The order and chemistry of amino acids cause each chain to fold into a shape. That shape helps determine how the protein behaves. When heat, acid, or another condition disrupts some of the forces holding the shape, a protein may unfold; this is called denaturation. Denaturation changes shape more than the chain's basic amino-acid sequence. Unfolded proteins can stick to one another; if they form a network or clumps, the result is coagulation.

Protein structure can be described at several levels: primary is the amino-acid sequence; secondary is local folding such as helices and sheets; tertiary is the overall three-dimensional fold of one chain; and quaternary is how multiple protein chains associate. Heat or acid can disrupt the forces maintaining folded shape without immediately cutting the peptide backbone. Some amino acids are “essential,” meaning the body cannot make enough and must obtain them from food. Milk proteins provide all nine essential amino acids, but that does not make dairy the only possible protein source. Milk has many proteins, but for the curd-mass event focus on two groups:

  • Caseins make up most of milk protein. They gather with calcium phosphate into small particles called casein micelles. Micelles are kept dispersed in normal milk by their surface chemistry.
  • Whey proteins remain in the watery part after casein curds form. Some whey proteins unfold with heat. They can affect foam and texture and, under some conditions, can be recovered as whey curds.

2. How acid makes curds

Fresh milk is slightly acidic, around pH 6.5. Adding acid or allowing bacteria to make acid lowers pH. As acidity increases, the electrical repulsion that helps keep casein micelles apart weakens. Calcium links within the micelle are also affected. Near the caseins' isoelectric region (about pH 4.6), the casein particles lose much of their net charge and can join into a network. The milk separates into a solid-rich curd and a liquid-rich whey.

In the event's curd task, drops of vinegar or lemon juice supply acid. Stirring distributes it; resting gives particles time to aggregate; cheesecloth separates the curds from liquid. Mass depends on the milk protein amount, milk type, acid amount, temperature, stirring, rest, and drainage. If those conditions vary between groups, a yield comparison is not fair.

Acid is not “cooking” the protein. It changes the chemical environment around casein so particles stop staying apart. Heat can also alter proteins, and heat plus acid can produce different textures than either alone.

3. Enzyme coagulation and cheese

Some cheeses are made with rennet enzymes. One milk enzyme source, chymosin, cuts a small exposed part of a stabilizing casein at the surface of the micelle. After that protective part is clipped, micelles can gather into a gel, even if the milk has not become strongly sour. Many cheeses combine enzyme coagulation with acidification, cutting the curd, draining whey, salting, and aging. Moisture, acidity, salt, microbes, and time help create different cheeses.

Acid-set curd and enzyme-set curd are therefore related but not identical methods: both bring casein together, but one shifts pH and the other changes a protein surface. In the Science Olympiad curd-mass task, acid addition is the central mechanism to explain.

4. Dairy and cooking chemistry

Heating can unfold whey proteins and change their interactions with casein, but milk proteins do not behave exactly like egg proteins. Milk can be heated without automatically forming a solid curd. Acid makes milk more likely to curdle when heated. Long heating also evaporates water and concentrates lactose and proteins; browning can occur when reducing sugars such as lactose react with amino groups in proteins (the Maillard reaction). This creates new color and aroma compounds.

  • Denaturation: protein loses some of its folded shape.
  • Coagulation: proteins associate into a network or clumps.
  • Curdling: visible clumping/separation in milk, often involving casein.
  • Fermentation: microbes use food molecules and produce new compounds, such as lactic acid from lactose.

5. Lactic-acid bacteria and probiotics

Starter cultures are selected microorganisms that reliably perform a useful fermentation. Lactic-acid bacteria use lactose and produce lactic acid. Lower pH gives yogurt its tart flavor and helps create its gel. Acid also makes conditions less favorable for many spoilage organisms, although fermented foods are not automatically sterile and still need safe handling and storage.

Probiotic is a more specific idea than “contains live bacteria”: it refers to live microorganisms that, in adequate amounts, confer a demonstrated health benefit. Not every fermented product, or every microbe in it, qualifies. For the event, know that cultures ferment lactose, create acidity and flavor, and may support probiotic products; avoid claiming all yogurt bacteria have the same proven health effect.

6. Milk allergy vs. lactose intolerance

Milk allergyLactose intolerance
Immune system reacts to one or more milk proteins, such as casein or whey.Digestive system lacks enough lactase enzyme to digest lactose efficiently.
Can cause serious allergic reactions; milk is a major food allergen under U.S. labeling rules.Undigested lactose can be fermented by gut microbes, causing gas, bloating, or diarrhea in some people.
Lactose-free cow's milk still contains milk proteins and is not safe for someone with milk allergy.Lactose-free milk has lactose split by lactase into simpler sugars; it remains dairy and still has milk proteins.

This distinction is essential: the allergen is milk protein, not lactose. A lactose-free label does not mean dairy-free or allergy-safe. Follow a person's medical guidance; this page is for learning, not diagnosis.
