Lesson 6 · Think like a food scientist

Plan a fair test, make measurements you can trust, and understand how the three tournament lab tasks connect to the course.

Big idea: A good experiment changes one thing on purpose, measures an outcome, and keeps other important conditions as similar as possible. A result is useful when you can explain how the procedure produced it.

1. Variables and controls

  • Question: what relationship are you testing? Example: “How does the amount of salt in the outside ice bath affect frozen ice-cream height?”
  • Independent variable: the one factor deliberately changed (salt amount).
  • Dependent variable: the outcome measured (self-standing ice-cream height after the event's specified wait).
  • Controlled variables: factors kept the same (mix volume, recipe, bag size, ice amount, shaking time, and measurement method).
  • Control condition: a baseline comparison, such as the current standard recipe or 0 mL salt. Not every investigation needs a separate “no treatment” control, but it needs a meaningful comparison.

Write your independent variable with its values and units. “Temperature” is vague; “starting cream temperature, 8°C, 13°C, 18°C” is clear. Change only that factor. If both salt and shake time change, you cannot tell which caused a difference.

2. Make results trustworthy

  1. Write the method before starting; measure amounts rather than estimating.
  2. Use the same equipment and order of steps each time.
  3. Repeat a condition when time and supplies allow. Replicates show whether a result is consistent.
  4. Record all observations, including failed trials. Do not erase inconvenient data.
  5. Record units and precision. A kitchen scale that reads whole grams cannot justify a result to 0.01 g.
  6. Make a data table with the independent variable in one column and outcome(s) in another.
  7. Graph the relationship when appropriate. Put the independent variable on the horizontal axis and dependent variable on the vertical axis.
  8. End with a conclusion that uses the data, states whether the prediction was supported, and names limits or likely sources of error.

3. Tournament task: ice cream formation

Rules outline a small milk/cream/half-and-half mixture with a limited amount of sugar and vanilla, placed inside a pint bag. The outer gallon bag contains ice and a measured amount of salt. The team shakes the bags for several minutes, then forms the product on a surface and records its height after it stands by itself for the required interval. The exact allowed quantities, time, and scoring procedure come from that season's rules.

Science link: dissolved salt lowers the freezing point of the water around ice; melting ice takes heat from the dairy mix. Shaking increases contact and moves warmer mixture to the cold surface. The mix's sugar, milk solids, fat, and air affect how much freezes and how it holds shape.

Possible investigation: vary one outer-bath salt amount; keep ice mass, inner-mix recipe, bag sizes, shaking time, and height measurement constant. Another question could vary sugar amount, but keep the bath and procedure constant.

4. Tournament task: curd mass

A measured amount of room-temperature milk is weighed. Vinegar or lemon juice is added in drops; the mixture is stirred for a fixed time and rests so casein can aggregate. It is drained through cheesecloth for the assigned interval and the curds are weighed. Record exact starting milk mass, acid type and amount, rest/drain time, and final curd mass.

Science link: acid lowers pH; casein micelles lose some of the forces that keep them apart and aggregate. Curd mass is affected by casein amount, acid dose and distribution, temperature, rest, and how much liquid remains trapped in the curd.

Possible investigation: vary the number of vinegar drops while holding the milk, temperature, stirring, resting, cloth, and draining time constant. Use a scale consistently and state if you measured mass in grams.

5. Tournament task: milk-fat mass / butter

A measured amount of heavy cream is placed in a lidded container and shaken until butter particles form. The liquid buttermilk is drained; the butter is weighed. The rules provide the allowed cream amount and container size. Record cream mass, temperature, shaking time, visible endpoint, and butter mass.

Science link: cream is an oil-in-water emulsion: fat globules are dispersed in watery serum. Agitation damages the globule membranes so fat droplets collide and stick together. Once enough fat joins into larger clusters, the emulsion breaks; fat becomes the continuous phase and watery buttermilk is expelled. This is phase inversion—from oil droplets in water to water droplets in a fat-rich continuous phase.

Possible investigation: compare starting cream temperatures while keeping cream brand/amount, container, shaking pattern, and endpoint method the same. Temperature changes the balance between solid and liquid milkfat and can influence how it churns.

6. Notebook requirements and scoring

Under the event rules described on this site, a team prepares a notebook before tournament day: at least three experiment entries for each of the three tasks, each recorded on the current official Food Science Experiment Form. Each entry must investigate one independent variable. More than nine experiments may be included, but only the first three for each task are graded. Check the current season's manual and the supervisor's instructions before submitting.

The notebook is impounded for grading. A clean entry should make it possible for another student to repeat the experiment: question, hypothesis, independent/dependent/controlled variables, materials, procedure, raw measurements with units, data analysis, and conclusion. Use the official required form; the site's printable sheet is a practice organizer only.

The scoring summary in the rules: notebook up to 18 points; each lab task can earn up to 10 points; written-test points are based on percent correct (up to 50); penalties can reduce the score. For mass-based tasks, compare the team's value to the supervisor's reference as specified in the rules. For the ice-cream task, the scoring uses measured height and rule-defined rounding. The exact formulas and rounding at a tournament should be taken from the current rule manual.

7. Safety and preparation

For home practice, an adult should supervise heating and food handling. Keep raw/uncooked mixtures away from tasting; a competition sample is an experimental material, not automatically food to eat. Wear the required eye protection and protective clothing listed in the current rules, tie back long hair, clean the work area, and dispose of materials safely. At the tournament, bring every team-supplied item required by the rules; supervisors do not necessarily provide the special bags, container, or cheesecloth.

Check yourself

  1. You vary cream temperature and measure butter mass. Identify the independent and dependent variables.
  2. Why must you keep shaking time and cream quantity the same?
  3. What is the purpose of repeating a trial?
  4. In churning, what happens to the two liquid phases?
  5. How many notebook experiments are required at minimum under the described rules?
  6. Why should you use the season's official experiment form and rules?
Answer check: Temperature is independent, butter mass is dependent; controlling the other factors makes a fair comparison; repeats help identify variability; the emulsion breaks and changes from fat droplets in watery liquid to a fat-rich continuous phase with watery droplets / expelled buttermilk; at least 3 per task × 3 tasks = 9; the event can update requirements and scoring between seasons.
