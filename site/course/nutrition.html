Lesson 5 · Dairy nutrition & deficiencies

Know what nutrients dairy can contribute, how recommended amounts differ from label percentages, and what deficiency questions are asking.

Big idea: Dairy foods can contribute protein, calcium, phosphorus, riboflavin, vitamin B12, and (when fortified) vitamin D. The amount depends on the product and serving. A food can be nutritious without being the only way to get a nutrient.

1. Nutrients and what the body uses them for

  • Protein: supplies amino acids used to build and repair body proteins, including muscle, enzymes, and many cell structures. Milk proteins include casein and whey.
  • Calcium: a mineral used in bones and teeth and also in muscle contraction, nerve signaling, and other body functions.
  • Vitamin D: helps the body absorb calcium and supports bone mineralization. Cow's milk in the U.S. is often fortified, but check the label—fortification and amounts vary.
  • Riboflavin (vitamin B2): helps cells use energy from food and supports normal growth and cell function. Milk is a useful source and riboflavin contributes to the greenish tint sometimes seen in whey.
  • Vitamin B12: helps maintain blood and nerve cells and supports DNA production; animal foods, including dairy, naturally provide it.
  • Phosphorus and potassium: minerals that support bones and many cell functions; dairy can contribute both.
  • Vitamin A: supports vision and immune function; it is fat-soluble and present in dairy fat, while some milk products are fortified.

2. Recommended amounts for a common competition age range

The table uses NIH Office of Dietary Supplements reference amounts for ages 9–13, a common age band for Division B students. RDAs are daily targets for people of a particular age/life stage. The values are not a prescription for an individual and are not a dairy serving recommendation.

Vitamin D15 µg/day (600 IU) (RDA)Often added to cow's milk; naturally present in few foodsDeficiency can cause rickets in children and poor bone mineralization.
Riboflavin (B2)0.9 mg/day (RDA)Milk and yogurt are useful sourcesDeficiency can cause cracks/sores around the mouth, inflamed lips or tongue, skin problems, and—in severe prolonged cases—anemia.
Vitamin B121.8 µg/day (RDA)Milk, yogurt, and cheese naturally contain B12Deficiency can cause megaloblastic anemia, tiredness, and nerve problems.
Phosphorus1,250 mg/day (RDA)Milk products provide phosphorus along with calcium and proteinSevere deficiency is uncommon from diet alone; it can disrupt bones, energy metabolism, and other body functions.
Vitamin A600 µg RAE/day (RDA)Present in milkfat; some reduced-fat products are fortifiedProlonged severe deficiency can impair low-light vision (night blindness) and increase infection risk.
Iodine120 µg/day (RDA)Milk and yogurt can contribute; amounts varyDeficiency can enlarge the thyroid (goiter) and reduce thyroid hormone production.

For ages 14–18 some values differ: calcium remains 1,300 mg, vitamin D 15 µg, riboflavin 1.0 mg for girls / 1.3 mg for boys, B12 2.4 µg, and phosphorus 1,250 mg per day. Iodine is 150 µg. Potassium AI is 3,000 mg for boys and 2,300 mg for girls. Always specify the age range and whether the reference is an RDA or AI.

3. RDA is not the same as %DV

The RDA is tailored to a life-stage group. The Daily Value (DV) is a standard reference amount used on U.S. Nutrition Facts labels so people can compare products. %DV tells what fraction of that label reference one serving provides. FDA's quick guide says 5% DV or less is low and 20% DV or more is high for a nutrient per serving.

Example: if a serving has 260 mg calcium, then relative to a 1,300 mg reference it provides 20%. For a 9–13-year-old, that matches the calcium RDA in this simple example, but on a package the displayed %DV uses FDA's standard label value. For nutrients where a child's RDA differs from the label DV, those percentages need not match. In actual competition, read the question carefully: a food label question asks for the declared amount/%DV; an RDA question asks for a specific age/life stage.

4. Compare product types carefully

  • Whole vs reduced-fat milk: removing fat lowers energy and fat; it does not automatically remove protein, lactose, or most minerals. Fat-soluble vitamins can change, and vitamins may be added back—read the actual label.
  • Fat-free milk: very little milkfat; it still contains milk proteins and lactose unless specially treated.
  • Lactose-free milk: lactose is split into simple sugars; calcium and protein can remain similar to regular milk, but it is not safe for milk allergy.
  • Plant-based drinks: composition differs by ingredient and brand; some are fortified, some are not. Check protein, calcium, vitamin D, sugar, and allergen information.
  • Yogurt and cheese: retain protein and minerals, but processing and draining change water, lactose, salt, and nutrient concentration per serving.

5. Benefits without overclaiming

Dairy can be a convenient source of protein and several bone-related nutrients. Those nutrients also occur in other foods, and fortified alternatives may contribute them too. A single product does not guarantee a healthy diet; the whole eating pattern and a person's needs matter. Do not claim that milk “prevents” every disease or that everyone must drink it. For competition answers, link a nutrient to its known role and give a cautious consequence of deficiency.
