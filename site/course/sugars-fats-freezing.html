Lesson 2 · Sugars, fats & freezing

Connect molecular structure to sweetness, texture, energy, butter making, and ice cream formation.

Big idea: A food's molecules and their shapes determine what they do. Small dissolved molecules change freezing behavior; fat structure affects whether fat is solid or fluid; and churning changes how the fat phase is organized.

1. Carbohydrates: sugar units and chains

Carbohydrates contain carbon, hydrogen, and oxygen. Monosaccharides are single sugar units such as glucose and galactose. Disaccharides are two linked sugar units. Lactose is a disaccharide made from glucose and galactose. Sucrose (table sugar) is glucose joined to fructose. Polysaccharides are long chains: starch is a storage carbohydrate; cellulose is a structural carbohydrate humans cannot digest.

Milk's main carbohydrate is lactose. The enzyme lactase breaks its bond, producing glucose and galactose. Lactic-acid bacteria can use lactose as food and produce lactic acid. Lactose is less sweet and far less soluble than table sugar; when concentrated products cool, lactose can crystallize. Large crystals create a sandy texture in some ice creams or condensed milk. Sugar is not only sweet: dissolved sugar also affects freezing, viscosity, browning, and the way a frozen dessert feels. Starch is a long polymer made from many glucose units. Plant starch contains mostly relatively straight amylose chains and branched amylopectin; the ratio affects how starch thickens when heated with water. Cellulose is also made of glucose units, but their links make a strong plant-cell-wall fiber that humans cannot digest. In dairy, milk contains little starch unless a product has a starch-based thickener added.

2. Lipids, fatty acids and triglycerides

Lipids are a broad family of molecules that do not mix well with water. Most dietary fat is in the form of triglycerides: one glycerol backbone joined to three fatty acids. Each fatty acid is a long carbon chain with a carboxyl end.

  • Saturated fatty acid: carbon atoms along the chain have as many hydrogen atoms as possible; there are no carbon-carbon double bonds. Chains can pack closely, so fats rich in these chains tend to be firmer at a given temperature.
  • Unsaturated fatty acid: has one or more carbon-carbon double bonds. Natural cis double bonds put a bend in the chain, making close packing harder and often lowering the melting range.
  • Monounsaturated: one double bond. Polyunsaturated: more than one double bond.

Animal fats and vegetable oils are mixtures of many triglycerides, not single compounds. Their melting behavior depends on fatty-acid chain length, saturation, and arrangement. This is why butter softens over a temperature range instead of switching instantly from solid to liquid.

Essential fats

Humans cannot make certain polyunsaturated fatty acids in sufficient amounts, so they must come from food. The key examples are linoleic acid (omega-6) and alpha-linolenic acid (omega-3). They are present mainly in plant oils, nuts, and seeds; dairy fat contains a mixture of fatty acids but should not be taught as the only or best source. “Essential” means required in the diet, not that a single food is necessary.

3. How fat bonds form: esterification

A fatty acid has a carboxyl group (—COOH); glycerol has three hydroxyl groups (—OH). When these groups react, they can form ester bonds and release water. Joining one glycerol with three fatty acids creates a triglyceride and three water molecules. This bond-forming process is called esterification (more specifically, condensation/ester formation).

The reverse idea is hydrolysis: adding water can split ester bonds. Digestive enzymes called lipases help hydrolyze triglycerides into fatty acids and glycerol-related products. For competition, recognize the structural relationship and know that fats are built from glycerol + fatty acids; advanced reaction mechanisms are not needed.

4. Freezing point depression

Pure water freezes at 0°C under ordinary pressure. When a substance such as salt or sugar dissolves in water, the dissolved particles interfere with water molecules lining up into an ice crystal. The solution must be cooled further before it freezes. This is freezing point depression, a colligative property: it depends mainly on the number of dissolved particles, not simply their identity.

In a bag ice-cream method, salt dissolves in the thin liquid water on the ice surface. That salty brine has a freezing point below 0°C, so it can remain liquid while colder than ordinary ice water. As ice melts, it absorbs heat from the ice-cream mixture. Heat flows from the warmer mix to the colder salty ice bath; the mix loses energy and freezes. Salt does not magically “make cold”—the melting ice and heat transfer do the cooling, while salt lets the bath get colder before it freezes.

Keep the cause-and-effect straight: more dissolved salt in the ice bath can lower its freezing point, but salt quantity is not the only factor. Contact, bath temperature, bag leaks, shaking, ingredient ratios, and time also affect the result. Change one variable at a time in a fair experiment.

5. What gives ice cream its texture?

Ice cream is a complex frozen foam/emulsion containing three important structures: ice crystals (mostly frozen water), a concentrated unfrozen liquid containing sugar, milk solids and fat, and air cells incorporated during agitation. Small ice crystals feel smooth; large crystals feel icy or grainy. Air makes the product lighter. Fat and proteins contribute body and help stabilize structure.

  • More dissolved sugar usually makes the mix freeze at a lower temperature and leaves more liquid unfrozen at serving temperature; too much can make a dessert soft or syrupy.
  • More fat adds richness and can help produce a creamy body; too much churning or an unsuitable temperature can cause fat globules to clump toward butter.
  • Agitation while freezing helps distribute small ice crystals and traps air; slow freezing or repeated thaw/refreeze cycles allow crystals to grow.
  • Milk proteins, including heat-treated whey proteins, can help limit ice crystal growth and stabilize the structure.

Protein denaturation/coagulationHeat or acid changes protein shape and can make proteins join into a network.Casein curds after acid is added to milk; whey proteins unfold during heating.

Maillard browning and caramelization both create brown color but are not the same reaction: Maillard involves amino compounds and reducing sugars; caramelization is sugar transformation by heat. Browning depends on temperature, time, moisture, pH, and ingredients.
