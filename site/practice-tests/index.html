✏️ Food Science practice set

Try each question without notes. Open the answer only after writing your own explanation. These questions cover the published event topics, not every possible tournament question.

How to score: Give yourself one point only when your answer includes both the correct idea and a reason. Write a star beside any missed item, revisit the linked lesson, then retry later without looking.

Part A · Milk chemistry & structure

1. Name the major components of cow's milk and explain why milk is more than a simple solution.

Water, lactose, milkfat, proteins, and minerals. Lactose/minerals dissolve; casein micelles are colloidal particles; fat droplets form an oil-in-water emulsion.

2. What makes lactose a disaccharide? What does lactase do?

Lactose consists of glucose linked to galactose. Lactase breaks that link to produce the two simpler sugars.

3. A triglyceride is built from which parts? What is an ester bond?

One glycerol and three fatty acids. Each fatty acid links to glycerol through an ester bond formed between a fatty-acid carboxyl group and a glycerol hydroxyl group, releasing water in the condensation reaction.

4. Distinguish saturated from unsaturated fatty acids by structure and likely packing.

Saturated chains have no carbon-carbon double bonds and can pack closely. Unsaturated chains have one or more double bonds; natural cis bonds bend chains and tend to impede close packing.

5. What does “essential fatty acid” mean? Name one omega-3 and one omega-6 example.

The body cannot make enough, so diet must supply it. Alpha-linolenic acid (omega-3) and linoleic acid (omega-6) are examples.

Part B · Dairy processes & lab tasks

6. Explain freezing point depression and how salt helps the bag ice-cream task.

Dissolved particles lower water's freezing temperature. Salt makes a brine that can remain liquid below 0°C; melting ice absorbs heat from the mix, while shaking transfers heat and increases contact. Salt itself does not create cold.

7. Name the three structures in ice cream and explain how large ice crystals affect texture.

Ice crystals, concentrated unfrozen mix, and air cells. Large crystals feel coarse or icy; small crystals feel smoother.

8. Compare casein and whey. Which usually forms acid curds?

Caseins gather in micelles and aggregate as pH falls; whey proteins mostly remain in the liquid phase after curd formation. Casein is the principal acid-curd-forming protein group.

9. What does denaturation mean? Is it identical to protein hydrolysis?

Denaturation is a change/unfolding of a protein's structure. Hydrolysis breaks covalent bonds in the chain using water; they are different processes.

10. How does acid make milk curdle? What is the liquid left after draining called?

Lower pH weakens the forces that keep casein micelles apart so casein aggregates into a network. The remaining liquid is whey.

11. Describe what happens when cream is churned into butter.

Agitation disrupts milkfat globule membranes; fat droplets aggregate and the emulsion breaks/phase-inverts. Butter is a fat-rich continuous phase with small water droplets; the expelled liquid is buttermilk.

12. Give the purpose of pasteurization and distinguish it from homogenization.

Pasteurization is a controlled heat treatment to reduce pathogens and spoilage microbes. Homogenization breaks fat globules into smaller droplets to slow cream separation; it is a physical dispersion change.

13. Name two ways dairy foods are preserved besides pasteurization.

Any two: refrigeration, UHT plus aseptic packaging, drying, evaporation/concentration, fermentation/acidification, or protective packaging and clean handling.

14. How do lactic-acid bacteria change milk? What is a probiotic?

They ferment lactose into lactic acid and other products, lowering pH and changing flavor/texture. A probiotic is a live microorganism shown to provide a health benefit when consumed in adequate amounts; not every fermented food automatically qualifies.

Part C · Nutrition, safety & labels

15. Name two differences between milk allergy and lactose intolerance.

Allergy is an immune reaction to milk proteins and can be severe; intolerance is difficulty digesting lactose due to insufficient lactase and is not an immune allergy. Lactose-free dairy still contains milk proteins and is not allergy-safe.

16. For a 9–13-year-old, what is the RDA for calcium? What vitamin helps absorb calcium, and what deficiency disease can it cause in children?

Calcium RDA 1,300 mg/day. Vitamin D helps calcium absorption; deficiency can cause rickets.

17. What do riboflavin and vitamin B12 do? State one deficiency effect for either.

Riboflavin (B2) helps energy metabolism and normal cell function; deficiency may cause mouth/lip sores or skin problems. B12 supports blood and nerve cells and DNA production; deficiency may cause megaloblastic anemia or nerve problems.

18. What does 20% DV mean on one serving? Is it always exactly 20% of a particular student's RDA?

The serving provides 20% of FDA's standard Daily Value for that nutrient. It may not equal 20% of a specific student's life-stage RDA because label DV is standardized while RDA can vary with age and life stage.

19. A product has 8% DV calcium. By FDA's quick guide, is it low or high?

Neither under the quick guide: 5% or less is low; 20% or more is high.

20. What nutrient source is a milk allergen? Does a “lactose-free” statement remove that allergen?

Milk proteins are the allergen; lactose-free treatment removes/splits the sugar, not milk proteins.

21. What organic ingredient percentages correspond to “organic” and “made with organic ingredients” in USDA categories?

At least 95% for “organic”; at least 70% for “made with organic …” (excluding water and salt and subject to other rules). The USDA seal is not allowed on “made with” products.

22. Why should a student compare labels before assuming soy, almond, oat, or rice beverage is nutritionally like cow's milk?

Plant-based drinks differ in protein, energy, fat, sugars, and fortification; check the actual Nutrition Facts and ingredients.

Part D · Experimental reasoning

23. In an ice-cream study, you change salt in the outer bag and measure standing height. Identify the independent and dependent variables and name two controls.

Independent: salt amount. Dependent: standing height. Controls could include ice mass, recipe and volume, bag size, starting temperature, shaking time, and measurement procedure.

24. Why can wet curds weigh more even if the same amount of casein formed?

They may retain more whey/water. Drain time, cloth, and handling must be controlled to compare curd mass fairly.

25. You compare butter output but vary both cream temperature and shake time. What is the problem?

Two variables changed, so a difference cannot be attributed to either one. Repeat with one independent variable at a time.

26. Write the equation for percent mass recovery if 18 g butter came from 45 g cream.

(18 g ÷ 45 g) × 100 = 40%. This is mass recovered relative to starting cream mass, not the cream's percent fat.
