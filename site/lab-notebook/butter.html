🧈 Lab Task: Milk Fat Mass

Shake heavy cream until it separates into butter and liquid buttermilk, then measure the recovered butter.

Safety: An adult supervises home practice. Use a sound lidded container; do not overfill it. Wear required competition safety clothing. Do not eat experiment samples. Check the current manual for exact quantities and scoring.

Why this works

Cream is a watery liquid with milkfat droplets dispersed through it. Each droplet has a membrane that helps it stay separate. Shaking repeatedly brings droplets together and damages their protective surfaces. Fat droplets aggregate, eventually forming a connected fat-rich mass. The water-based liquid is expelled as buttermilk. In emulsion language, churning changes an oil-in-water emulsion toward a water-in-oil structure: the fat becomes continuous and tiny water droplets remain inside it.

Temperature matters because milkfat is a mixture of triglycerides that melt across a range. If it is too warm, the fat may be too liquid and make greasy butter; if too cold, too much fat is solid and it may churn slowly. The tournament procedure's target is around 13°C.

Rule-based starting procedure

  1. Measure about 150 mL heavy cream. Use the current manual's permitted product and amount.
  2. Weigh the cream and record its starting mass.
  3. Bring the cream to around 13°C (55°F), the target in the supplied rule summary, and write down the actual temperature.
  4. Transfer to the required 500–600 mL lidded container, close securely, and shake consistently.
  5. Continue until visible butter particles form and liquid separates. Record elapsed time and observations at regular intervals.
  6. Drain off the buttermilk as directed and weigh the butter. Keep the drain method consistent in every trial.

Do not put two changed factors on one form. For instance, if one trial uses warmer cream and more shaking, any difference has two possible explanations.

Calculate and explain

Compare recovered butter mass with the starting cream mass, but remember cream contains water, proteins, lactose, and minerals as well as fat. Therefore, butter mass will be less than cream mass, and not all starting fat is necessarily recovered. If the event asks for percentage mass recovery, use:

Mass recovery (%) = (recovered butter mass ÷ starting cream mass) × 100.
Use the units consistently, such as grams divided by grams. This is a mass ratio, not the percent milkfat in the original cream.

The event score may compare your value with the supervisor's reference rather than simply reward the largest butter mass. Read the current scoring rules before drawing conclusions.
