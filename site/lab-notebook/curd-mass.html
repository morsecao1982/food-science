🧀 Lab Task: Curd Mass

Measure the solid curd formed when acid is added to milk.

Safety: Adult supervision for handling liquids and equipment. Wear required eye protection/clothing. Do not taste laboratory samples. Use the current event manual for exact allowed materials, amounts, and scoring.

Why this works

Milk contains casein proteins gathered into micelles. Acid lowers pH, changing the charges and associations that keep the micelles dispersed. The casein particles join into a network called curd; liquid whey drains away. More curd mass can result from more casein in the starting milk, but also from trapped water, so drainage method must be controlled.

Rule-based starting procedure

  1. Bring milk to room temperature. Measure 120 mL (or the season's specified amount); weigh the milk as required and record the mass in grams.
  2. Add the assigned acid (vinegar or lemon juice) drop by drop. Write down the exact acid and drop count/volume.
  3. Stir for 30 seconds using a consistent motion.
  4. Let the mixture rest for about 5 minutes so curds can form.
  5. Pour through cheesecloth and drain for 15 minutes using the same setup each trial.
  6. Weigh the collected curds. Record final mass and any observation such as clear/cloudy whey, small flakes, or a compact curd.

Plan notebook trials

Use one independent variable per form. Possible series: acid type (vinegar or lemon juice), acid amount, milk fat level/type, or fixed drain time. Never change acid amount and drain time together if you want to identify a cause. Each student/team should submit their actual results, not predicted numbers copied from this page.

Data and interpretation

  • Record initial milk mass, not only volume, if the form asks for mass.
  • If using repeated trials, calculate mean curd mass and note the range (maximum minus minimum).
  • Explain whether the sample might retain more whey. Wet curds can weigh more even if they contain the same amount of casein.
  • A fair conclusion names the independent variable and compares actual measured results.

Example of good reasoning: “With more acid, curd mass increased between our first and second levels, then changed little at the highest level. This is consistent with more casein aggregating as pH fell, but curd wetness and the visible endpoint may also have affected mass.”
